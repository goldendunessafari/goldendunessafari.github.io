Desert Safari from Abu Dhabi

A visitor guide to the Al Khatim dunes — what the trip is really like, and how to pick the right one.

Abu Dhabi has its own desert, and it is not the one most visitors are shown. The dunes at Al Khatim lie about forty-five minutes east of the city: quieter than the stretches Dubai operators use, softer underfoot, and on a weekday almost empty.

This page describes what actually happens on a trip out there, so you can decide whether it is for you before you book anything.

The drive out

A driver collects you in the city and takes the E22 east. The built-up edge of Abu Dhabi thins fast — within half an hour there is scrub, then nothing but sand. At the edge of the dunes the driver stops to let air out of the tyres. It looks like a delay; it is the reason the vehicle floats over soft sand instead of digging into it.

What you will actually do

Dune bashing

Twenty to thirty minutes of driving up, along and down the faces of the dunes. This is the part people either love or quietly endure, and it is entirely adjustable. Tell the driver before you set off if you want it gentle — not halfway through. A driver who runs these dunes daily can make the same route feel like a fairground ride or a slow roll over hills.

Camel riding

Short and slow, usually a loop near the camp. The camels are calmer than they look and the handlers lead them on foot. It is the one activity that works equally well for a nervous seven-year-old and a grandparent.

Sandboarding

A board, a low dune, and as many runs as you have energy for. Nobody is good at it immediately, which is most of the fun. Closed shoes make a real difference here.

The camp

On evening trips you arrive as the light drops, and the rest of the night is grilled food, Arabic coffee, henna and live performances under the open sky. Once the generator noise is behind you, the quiet out there is genuinely striking. Morning trips skip the camp meal and have you back in the city before lunch.

Which trip suits whom

  • Morning. Cooler, shorter, no dinner. Easiest option with small children, and the dunes are freshly combed by the overnight wind with no tyre tracks in them.
  • Evening. The light everyone comes for, plus dinner and the show. The standard choice for a first visit.
  • Overnight. You stay after the day visitors leave. The sky out there, away from city light, is the reason to do it.
  • Private. Your own vehicle and driver, your own pace. Worth it for families and anyone who wants to stop for photographs whenever they like.
  • Self-drive. You meet the convoy in your own car. The cheapest way to see the dunes if you are already mobile.

When to go

October to March is the comfortable window: daytime temperatures in the twenties, and evenings cool enough that a light jacket is welcome at the camp. Both morning and evening trips work well in those months.

June to September, the middle of the day is hard work out there. Evening trips are the sensible choice in summer, because you reach the dunes as the ground is cooling rather than at its worst. There is a fuller month-by-month breakdown on the seasons page.

What to bring

  • Closed shoes rather than sandals — sand at forty degrees does not forgive bare feet.
  • Loose clothing with long sleeves. More comfortable than it sounds, in both heat and evening cold.
  • Sunglasses, and a light jacket between December and February.
  • More water than you think you need.
  • If anyone gets carsick on ordinary roads, treat it before you leave rather than after.

Before you book

Pickup windows shift with the season and each package includes a different set of activities, so it is worth reading the safari packages and pickup times before you plan the rest of your day around the trip.

A few questions worth asking whoever you book with: is hotel pickup included or is it a meeting point, are child seats available, and how long is the whole trip door to door. Honest operators answer all three without hedging.
